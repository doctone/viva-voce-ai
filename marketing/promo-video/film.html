The Causes of the First World War

The outbreak of the First World War in 1914 cannot be attributed to a single cause; rather, it emerged from a complex interplay of militarism, alliances, imperialism and nationalism. Historians have long debated the relative weight of each factor, yet it is clear that the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo acted as a catalyst rather than a root cause.

Firstly, the system of alliances transformed a regional dispute into a continental conflict. The Triple Alliance and the Triple Entente bound the great powers together in a web of obligations, meaning that Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia rapidly drew in Russia, Germany, France and ultimately Britain. Furthermore, the rigidity of mobilisation timetables, most notably the Schlieffen Plan, reduced the space available for diplomacy.

Secondly, militarism fostered a culture in which war was seen as both inevitable and desirable. The Anglo-German naval race, beginning with the launch of HMS Dreadnought in 1906, heightened mutual suspicion. Military leaders exercised considerable influence over civilian governments, and public opinion was shaped by a press that frequently glorified conflict.

Moreover, imperial rivalry created flashpoints across the globe. The Moroccan Crises of 1905 and 1911 demonstrated the fragility of great-power relations and deepened the antagonism between Germany and the Entente powers. In the Balkans, the decline of the Ottoman Empire left a vacuum that Austria-Hungary and Russia each sought to fill.

In conclusion, while the assassination provided the spark, the underlying tensions of the period had already created a situation in which a general war was highly likely. It is therefore reasonable to argue that responsibility was shared, though some historians, notably Fritz Fischer, have placed particular emphasis on German war aims.

Nationalism, too, played a decisive role. Pan-Slavism encouraged Russian support for Serbia, while in France the desire to recover Alsace-Lorraine lingered after 1871. Within Austria-Hungary, competing nationalities threatened the integrity of the empire, making its leaders more willing to gamble on a decisive confrontation.

Was the Treaty of Versailles the main cause of the Second World War?

Student: Daniel Okoro · Submitted 14 March

The Treaty of Versailles created a resentment so deep that German democracy never recovered from it. The War Guilt Clause, Article 231, was felt as a national humiliation, and the reparations of 132 billion gold marks were, in Keynes's words, economically impossible.

However, it would be too simple to argue that Versailles alone made war inevitable. The Great Depression, rather than the treaty, gave the Nazi party its mass support after 1930, and the policy of appeasement allowed Hitler to dismantle the settlement piece by piece.

Ultimately, Versailles was a necessary but not sufficient cause: it created the grievance, but it took economic collapse and diplomatic weakness to turn that grievance into war.
